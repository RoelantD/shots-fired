# The AI Agreed With Me. That Should Scare You.

*A reality check on vibe coding your way into a worse situation*

---

We run a user group. Nothing glamorous. We needed a website.

Years ago we landed on Hugo. None of us had used it before. We figured it out, shipped something, it worked. Job done.

Fast forward to 2025. I figured this was a good use case for AI-assisted development. The site is simple, the codebase is small, and I know what I want. I am a C# developer. Razor Pages would be fine. Something on the .NET stack. Familiar, maintainable, boring in the best possible way.

So I asked.

What came back was not Razor Pages. It was a full CMS migration. A different static site generator with its own conventions, its own templating language, its own quirks. A lateral move dressed up as progress. I would have spent a weekend learning a new tool to end up with something functionally identical to what I already had.

Not better. Just different. And different has a cost.

> "A lateral move dressed up as progress. I would have spent a weekend learning a new tool to end up with something functionally identical to what I already had."

---

Here is where it gets interesting.

When I pushed back and said this was not an improvement, the AI agreed. Immediately. Confidently. It acknowledged the whole thing was a lateral move and started suggesting the .NET approach I had in mind from the start.

Which raises the obvious question: why did it not suggest that in the first place?

Because it did not know what I actually valued. I had not told it. I said "update the site." I did not say "keep the operational overhead low" or "stay on the stack I already know." I gave it a task without context, and it filled in the blanks with reasonable-sounding defaults.

And when I provided context in the form of disagreement, it updated. That is actually the system working as intended. The problem is not that the AI changed its mind. The problem is that I could have just said yes to the first answer and walked away thinking I had done something good.

> "The problem is not that the AI changed its mind. The problem is that I could have just said yes to the first answer and walked away thinking I had done something good."

---

This is not a doom post.

AI coding assistance is genuinely useful. I use it constantly. But there is a mode of using it that feels productive and is not. It is the mode where you describe a task, accept the output, and move on. Where the tool provides the thinking so you do not have to. Where the velocity feels real but the decisions are hollow.

The AI does not know your constraints unless you tell it. It does not know your team's skill set, your maintenance bandwidth, your preference for boring technology that works. It will produce something coherent and plausible and potentially worse than what you had.

And if you do not know enough to evaluate the output, you will not catch it.

> "The AI does not know your constraints unless you tell it. It will produce something coherent and plausible and potentially worse than what you had."

---

The skill that matters right now is not prompting. It is judgment.

You need to be able to look at what comes back and ask: is this actually better, or does it just look like progress? Does this solve my problem, or does it introduce a new one? Would I have made this choice myself, given an hour to think about it?

If the answer is no, push back. The AI will fold. That is the point. It is a tool that responds to pressure. The pressure has to come from you having an opinion worth pushing with.

> "The skill that matters right now is not prompting. It is judgment."

Hugo was fine. The new thing might be fine too. But "fine" is not a reason to migrate. You need an actual reason, and that reason has to be yours.

> "It is a tool that responds to pressure. The pressure has to come from you having an opinion worth pushing with."

Think first. Then prompt.
